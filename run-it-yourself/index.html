How to use it

  1. Run it. Press ▶ RUN. With nothing changed you get the video's exact result: 699,712 worlds survive.
  2. Try an experiment. Click any card under Experiments. It sets the odds and runs straight away.
  3. Change the odds. Drag a slider, or open All 51 forks and type any odd. Then press ▶ RUN.
  4. Share it. Copy link puts your exact settings in a link. Anyone who opens it can run the same scenario.

Every simulated Cold War runs from July 1945 to December 1991 through the same 51 real crises, false alarms and accidents. At each one the computer rolls a die against an odd. Most rolls go the way history went; some don't. Before 1955, when the Soviet Union had under 200 warheads, a war between the superpowers counts as a one-sided atomic war rather than World War III.

Every world has its own fixed dice (seed 19450716), so a run is repeatable: the same settings always give the same numbers, on any computer.

Questions

Is this really the model from the video?

Yes. The code is the video's model (version 4, locked 30 October 2026) line for line; only the part that writes result files on a computer is left out. With nothing changed, this page gives the video's numbers exactly.

Where do the odds come from?

They are judgements, written down openly, built from what historians have found in the archives. No source gives these exact numbers. Every fork has its reasons and sources on the sources page; every odd, in all three columns, is in the odds table.

What do the three columns mean?

Safe follows historians who think the danger was exaggerated (Tertrais, 2017). Middle is the video. Dangerous follows those who think we got very lucky (Chatham House, 2014; Lundgren, 2013).

What does “secret incidents × 2” do?

Most known false alarms and accidents come from declassified American files; the Soviet ones are mostly still sealed. This doubles the chance of every false alarm and accident going wrong, as if there were twice as many we don't know about.

Why does changing one odd change other forks' numbers?

A world that ends early never reaches the later crises. Make 1948 more dangerous and fewer worlds are left to end in 1962.

What counts as “survived”?

Reaching 26 December 1991, the end of the Soviet Union, without a war between the superpowers. A world can survive even if a nuclear weapon was used in a smaller war; the results show how many stayed completely quiet, like ours.

Does it send my settings anywhere?

No. Everything runs in your browser. A shared link only carries the settings themselves.
